How to Check Website Traffic: The Honest Guide

Learn how to check website traffic properly — your own with Analytics and Search Console, and a competitor's with estimation tools — and what each method can and cannot tell you.

Your own site: use your own data

The only accurate way to check traffic for a site you own is to use data collected on that site. Google Analytics 4 shows how many people visit, which pages they view, where they come from, and what they do. Google Search Console shows how often your pages appear in Google search, how often they are clicked, and which queries bring people in. Together they give a complete picture: Search Console tells you about search visibility, Analytics tells you about everything that happens after the click. Both are free, and both should be installed on every site you care about. If you own a site and do not have them installed, that is the first thing to fix.

A competitor's site: only estimates exist

For a site you do not own, there is no way to see its real traffic. The owner has the only accurate numbers, and they are private. What public tools offer are estimates built from panels, browser data, search data, and statistical models. SimilarWeb, Semrush, and Ahrefs all publish such estimates, and they are useful for comparing sites roughly, but they are often wrong for small sites, where the sample is thin. Treat any number for a competitor as an order of magnitude, not a fact. A tool that claims a small blog gets exactly 4,213 visits a month is presenting a model output as if it were a measurement, and that is misleading.

What estimation tools actually do

Traffic estimation tools combine several signals. They look at a site's search rankings and estimate clicks from them, they use data from browser extensions and panels, they look at backlinks and social signals, and they compare the site with similar sites whose traffic is known. The model then produces an estimate, usually as a range. The more signals a site has — high search visibility, many backlinks, a large audience — the more reliable the estimate tends to be. For a brand-new or very small site, the estimate is mostly a guess, and different tools will disagree wildly. That disagreement is itself useful information: it tells you the site is too small to measure from outside.

How to use estimates sensibly

Use estimates for relative comparisons, not absolute numbers. If one competitor is estimated at ten times the traffic of another across several tools, that gap is probably real, even if both numbers are wrong. Look at trends over months rather than a single month, because trends are more stable than point estimates. Check the same site in two or three tools and see whether they agree. And always ask what the estimate is for: a site with high traffic but no relevance to your audience is less useful than a smaller site that reaches exactly the people you want. Estimates are a starting point for research, not a verdict.

Checking traffic the right way, step by step

If the site is yours, install Google Analytics 4 and Search Console today, verify ownership, and wait a few weeks for data to accumulate. Then look at users, sessions, and engaged sessions in Analytics, and clicks and impressions in Search Console. If the site belongs to someone else, run it through one or two estimation tools, note the range and the trend, and cross-check it against visible signals: how often the site publishes, how many comments or shares its posts get, and how strong its search presence is. Frequently asked questions

Can I see the exact traffic of any website?

No. Only the site owner has exact numbers. Public tools provide estimates based on models, and those estimates are rough, especially for small sites.

Is SimilarWeb accurate?

It is reasonably useful for large, popular sites and for relative comparisons. For small sites its estimates can be far from reality.

What is the best free way to check my own traffic?

Google Analytics 4 plus Google Search Console. Both are free and together they cover search visibility and on-site behaviour.
